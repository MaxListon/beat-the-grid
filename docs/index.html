Where it failed

The ten worst days, explained

Three of the worst came from two hurricanes: the weather forecast was right, but millions of customers lost power. Operators knew about the outages; a weather-driven model can't.

Can storm warnings fix this? An experiment

Hurricane, tropical-storm, storm-surge, extreme-wind and ice-storm warnings are public before a storm hits. I added two inputs from the National Weather Service archive: the share of a region's cities under a warning at issue time, and the share where one just ended. Only warnings already in effect at 12:00 UTC the day before count. Rerun through the same walk-forward backtest:

  • Error on the ~5,100 storm hours fell from 8.3% to 7.9%; ordinary days were unchanged.
  • Hurricane days improved by 13–35%: Dominion SC on Helene 30.9% → 20.2%, FPL on Milton 28.0% → 23.4%, Duke on Helene 30.0% → 26.1%.
  • The overall score barely moved (3.08% either way; one loss became a tie).

Not adopted yet. I thought of it after seeing these worst days, so its backtest gain is partly hindsight. It will be tested on new storms before it goes into the live model.

The live record

Forecasts published before the day begins

A backtest can be tuned until it looks good; a live record can't. Every morning at 12:00 UTC an automated job publishes tomorrow's forecast with a model frozen at launch and commits it to GitHub before the day begins.

Tables and columns

hourly: one row per scored region-hour (glitch hours removed, same rows as the headline). region, hour_ending_utc, local_date, local_hour, actual_mw, official_mw, ours_mw, ours_low_mw / ours_high_mw (80% range), blend_mw.

peaks: one row per region-day. region, local_date, actual_peak_mw / _hour, ours_peak_mw / _hour, official_peak_mw / _hour.

Errors are fractions (0.031 = 3.1%). Results show the first 500 rows; the CSV download has all of them.

Method

How the contest stays fair

Four rules, each enforced in code and covered by tests.

The rules
  • Issue time: each forecast covers one local calendar day and is issued at 12:00 UTC the day before (4 am Pacific, 7 am Eastern).
  • Data cutoff: the model may only use demand reported up to 06:00 UTC that morning. A test scrambles all later data and checks that nothing changes.
  • Weather: archived forecasts made at least 48 hours ahead, never observed weather.
  • Same hours: both forecasts are scored only where actual demand, the official forecast and mine all exist. Hours where either is off by more than 50% are reporting glitches and are dropped for both.
The model
  • One gradient-boosted model (LightGBM) shared by all 34 regions. It predicts demand as a ratio to the past week's average, so small and large regions learn from each other.
  • Inputs: recent demand, calendar and holidays, and population-weighted weather forecasts for each region's largest cities (temperature, humidity, cloud, wind, sunlight).
  • An 80% range for every hour from quantile models, calibrated per region with conformal prediction.
  • Settings were tuned on April–June 2024, a window that is never scored.
Testing it honestly
  • Walk-forward: retrained every quarter on the past only, then scored on the following three months it had never seen.
  • Significance: Diebold-Mariano test on daily errors, with Newey-West variance and Holm correction across regions. A "win" means the difference is very unlikely to be luck.
  • No cherry-picking: regions were chosen by data coverage alone, before scoring.
Limits I'd flag
  • Some operators may refresh their "day-ahead" forecast closer to real time, which would give them an edge in this comparison.
  • Weather comes from up to three cities per region. I tested five per region on the tuning window and it didn't help, so I kept three.
  • Archived humidity, cloud, wind and sunlight forecasts only exist from February 2024, so scoring starts in July 2024.
  • The live model has no outage signal, so storms that cut power (Hurricanes Helene and Milton) are its worst days. A storm-warning input helps (see “Where it failed”) but isn't adopted until it's tested on new storms.

Why I built this

Every US grid operator publishes a forecast of tomorrow's electricity demand, and the government makes all of it public. I wanted to know how good those forecasts really are, and whether one carefully built model, held to the information it would really have had, could match them.

The answer turned out more interesting than yes or no. My model wins where most electricity is used and loses in many smaller regions. It is weakest on the busiest days, which are the ones that matter most. And it is most useful alongside the operators' forecast, not instead of it.

I've tried to show all of that, including the days it failed badly. The part I'm proudest of is the live record: every forecast is published before the day begins and never edited, which is the one thing a backtest can't fake.
